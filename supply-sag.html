Supply sag: what the bench showed on one board

The field fault (a unit that stops answering the network until it is power-cycled) has now been reproduced on the bench with a programmable supply. A dip that pulls the board's 5 V rail into a narrow "no-man's-land" band leaves the Ethernet chip wedged — alive but deaf — until power is removed. We tested firmware, the 22 µF reset capacitor, and the board's reset line itself: none of them fix it. The fault is set by a slow-rising supply edge — a clean, fast power-up is always fine — so the fix is a small change on the power path that guarantees the board only ever powers up cleanly. Specific components are in bench validation.

Bench, 2026-10-27 and 2026-10-28 (Test 0) · one pilot box (firmware v3.3), RuiDeng RD6006 lab supply replacing the 10 V unit · supply controlled and the board read over the network · every trial reported

What we found

A supply sag into roughly 2.7–3.8 V on the 5 V rail puts the W5500 Ethernet chip into a "deaf" state that only a power cycle clears. Above that band the board rides through; a very deep, clean loss of power reboots it cleanly. The danger is a slow rise or drift that lingers in between.

This matches the field symptom exactly, and it matches the installation's wiring: the 10 V board supply shares its phase with solid-state relays that switch 30–40 switch-mode LED supplies each. The in-rush when those capacitor banks charge sags the shared phase at the very moment the box sequences its outputs on. The in-rush explanation is consistent with every bench result.

It cannot be fixed in firmware, on the reset line, or with the capacitor. Bench Test 0 (2026-10-28) showed that pulsing the reset does not recover the wedged chip, that holding the board in reset through the whole power-up ramp does not prevent it, and that the 22 µF capacitor makes no difference — the fault is independent of the reset line. Only removing power clears it, and a clean, fast power-up always starts correctly. The fix is therefore on the power path — see the hardware options.

The voltage danger band

The board runs from a 5 V regulator. Two parts have minimum voltages below which they misbehave; the chip's brown-out detector only forces a reset far lower, at a level the fuse reports as 2.7 V. Between the two sits a band where the parts run out of specification without a clean reset being forced — the recipe for a wedged chip.

Test 1 — power-up: rise time decides, and the capacitor does not save it

With the board powered only from the lab supply, we powered it down fully and brought it up at different rise times, checking each time whether it came up on the network. A near-instant step always worked; anything slower came up deaf. Fitting the original 22 µF reset capacitor did not change the slow-ramp result, and neither did a firmware change that waits for a healthy rail before starting the chip.

Test 2 — it's the rising edge, not the dip

Later, more controlled runs (2026-10-28, "Test 0") isolated what actually triggers the deaf state. The result is clear: the board is wedged by a slow rise of the rail through the danger band — not by how deep or how long a dip is. A dip that recovers quickly just brown-out reboots and comes back on its own.

What decides it

  • A fast dip to 4 V and straight back — any duration from near-zero up to ~0.8 s — simply brown-out reboots the board, which self-recovers in a few seconds. Depth and duration do not decide it.
  • A slow rise of the rail through the danger band (the power-up ramp above) is what reliably leaves it deaf.
  • Pulsing the reset line does not recover a wedged chip, and holding reset through the ramp does not prevent it (two trials each); the 22 µF capacitor makes no difference. The fault is on the power path, not the reset line.

Why firmware, the reset line and the capacitor can't fix it

Firmware can't

  • A chip soft-reset over the internal bus does not clear the wedged state (the firmware self-check already does that and still cannot recover it).
  • A build that waits for a healthy rail before starting the chip made no difference (0/3).
  • Only removing the chip's power clears it — which firmware cannot do.

The reset line can't

  • Test 0, power held: pulsing the reset (the button, and a direct short) rebooted the processor but did not bring the Ethernet chip back.
  • Holding the board in reset through the whole power-up ramp and releasing at full voltage still came up deaf (two trials).
  • So a reset-supervisor on the board reset line — our earlier candidate — cannot fix this.

The 22 µF capacitor can't

  • It is a fixed delay on the reset line; it only helps a fast power-on, by holding reset through a rise of a few milliseconds.
  • On a slow ramp the delay is used up long before the rail is healthy.
  • A direct A/B (cap removed vs refitted) made no difference to the slow-ramp deaf state or the running-dip reboot — so it is not the culprit, and it is kept (it is the USB reset capacitor).

The hardware fix

  1. Guarantee a clean, fast power-up (the direction we are taking). A power-path undervoltage-lockout (UVLO) load switch keeps the board unpowered until the supply is solidly up, then connects it hard — so the board always sees the fast, clean start it needs (an instant step was 8/8). It also disconnects on a deep sag, so recovery is a clean reconnect rather than a slow drift through the danger band. A reset-supervisor on the reset line was tried and ruled out (Test 0).
  2. Hold up or decouple the supply. A blocking diode plus bulk energy on the board's input bridges a dip; or feed the board from a source that is not the same phase the output SSRs switch, so the in-rush cannot sag it. The cleanest root-cause route where the wiring allows it.
  3. Future board revision. Bring the Ethernet chip's power (or reset) under a dedicated control, so a clean power-cycle of the chip can be forced. This needs a board redesign.

Specific components are in bench validation (continuing with different parts). The acceptance test: a clean network start on a slow power-up ramp, and clean recovery from a deep sag, on a modified unit — plus an on-site measurement of the real supply (below).

Is the in-rush theory still valid?

Yes — it is consistent with every result, and nothing contradicts it. The bench shows that a sag into 2.7–3.8 V on the 5 V rail produces exactly the field symptom, and that it only takes a dip of tens of milliseconds. The installation shares the board's 10 V supply with the phase whose SSRs switch 30–40 switch-mode LED supplies; the capacitor-charging in-rush of those supplies is a credible source of such a sag, timed to when the box sequences power on — which is when units are reported to drop.

What is not yet proven is the last link in the chain: that the in-rush really pulls the rail into the danger band in the field. That needs a direct measurement on site — an oscilloscope on the 10 V rail, or the board's own logged minimum supply, captured while the SSRs switch their real loads. Until then the in-rush is the leading, well-supported explanation, not a closed case.

Honest limits

  • One pilot box, on the bench, with a lab supply standing in for the installed 10 V unit. The ramp and dip shapes are controlled approximations, not a recording of the real supply.
  • The rail thresholds are datasheet/fuse values (the ATmega is specified ≥ 4.5 V for 16 MHz per its speed grade; the W5500 ≥ 2.97 V; brown-out fuse 2.7 V). The board's own 5 V/3.3 V rails were not scoped on the bench, so the danger band is inferred from the input behaviour; the deaf outcome and current draw are measured.
  • The field link — in-rush actually reaching the danger band on an installed system — is still to be measured on site.
  • Firmware v3.3 remains worthwhile: it rides through shallow sags and recovers link drops by itself. It simply cannot rescue the deep-sag deaf state, which is a hardware limit of this board.
