EPS firmware 3.3: executive summary

v3.3 is the firmware we recommend as the official EPS firmware. It replaces the network layer of v2.2 with one written for the job (v3.0), brings it in line with the network standards an enterprise network expects (v3.1), widens the chip self-check to the faults that reproduce the field dropout (v3.2), and closes the four gaps a full code analysis found in that self-check (v3.3). Where v2.2 needed a power cycle after the network failures we reproduced, v3.3 recovers by itself, measures its own supply, and records what happened when something goes wrong. It was tested overnight against every earlier version on the same box; it has not yet run in the field. The case for making v3.3 official → · All versions compared →

Status 2026-10-12 · Bench: three units on a NETGEAR M4250 (a switch model with field failures), then a real EPS box cabled to a PC · All tests pre-registered, every failure reported

Bottom line

Field problem: EPS units on v2.2 randomly drop off the network (no ping, no web page, no control) until their power is cycled.

What we found: v2.2 has several defects that lead to exactly that state. The clearest one: in DHCP mode, a single DHCP renewal that goes unanswered makes v2.2 restart itself, and if the DHCP server is away at that moment, the unit stays off the network until someone power-cycles it. Others: the network chip losing its settings (v2.2 never notices), and connections that block the control port. For units set to a static address, the field trigger is still open.

What the code analysis added (2026-10-12): v2.2 cannot silence a static unit by its own logic; something outside the firmware has to upset the network chip. But every such upset is permanent on v2.2, and the most likely trigger is the supply: the board's brown-out level is 2.7 V (the box reports it), well below what the CPU and the network chip need.

v3.3: recovers by itself from every one of these in our tests, typically within seconds, keeps the outputs on while it does, and keeps a log (now with the supply voltage at every chip fault) that will tell us what happened if a unit does drop off in the field. In the single-register fault matrix it recovers from all 17 faults, including two new ones: a PHY held in reset, on which v3.2 is dead, and a link that only the new link-down recovery can bring back. On the pre-registered suite of 56 network-standard requirements it passes 48 (v3.1/v3.2: 46, v2.2: 26); of its six failures, two are limits of the network chip and four are kept on purpose. We are confident v3.3 is the most robust firmware we have. Whether it ends the field dropouts will be shown by a pilot and a supply-sag test, not by the bench.

What changed from v2.2 to v3.3

Outputs, timing, inputs, LEDs, the TCP control protocol (Companion, eXcontrol, Crestron, Q-SYS) and all stored settings work as before. The network layer below was rewritten in v3.0, brought to standard in v3.1, had its self-check widened in v3.2, and had the gaps in that self-check closed in v3.3.

Why v3.3 over v3.2: a code analysis found that v3.2's self-check ignored the PHY's reset bit, that nothing recovered a link that stayed down, and that its deaf-receiver check could never fire on a network without a gateway or right after a link-up. v3.3 closes all of these, measures the supply, and sends one ARP request per check instead of twenty. In the overnight run it recovered from all 17 faults (v3.2 is dead on the PHY held in reset) and passed every regression test, Twice in 40 chip-reset trials the network chip came back deaf after its re-init and cleared by itself (38 s and about 3.5 min): the rare "deaf after a link negotiation" state, now captured on this box too. Faster detection of it is the next firmware step. Full argument: Why v3.3 should be official.

v3.0's one recorded miss: a single lost ping in 6 load-test runs. Every bug found during testing was fixed and is listed in the full report.

Why v3.3 still fails four standards

  • Network chip limits (2): the W5500 corrupts long ping payloads and can't reassemble fragmented packets. No firmware on this board can change that; v2.2 fails the same two.
  • Fixed in v3.3 (2): ARP requests to an absent gateway (now one per check) and 414 for an over-long URL. Left as is (1): no 400 when the Host header is missing, because control systems send hand-written HTTP strings that often omit it.
  • Kept on purpose (3, plus the Host check above): the web page and control systems switch power with simple GET links; a stalled receiver is dropped after 2 s so it can't hold up power control; while waiting for a DHCP server the unit asks from its fallback address, so it stays reachable.

Is v2.2 as good, since both fail four?

  • No. One failed item fails a whole standard, so the count of failed standards hides the size of the gap: v2.2 fails 25 requirements, v3.3 6.
  • Only v2.2 takes an IP address already in use, asks for DHCP once a second, renews and rebinds incorrectly, drops TCP connections after 18 s, and treats every HTTP method as a GET.
  • The standards suite doesn't test recovery from the field failures, where the gap is largest (table above).

What we can say, and what we can't yet

We can say

  • v3.3 recovers by itself, in seconds, from every v2.2 failure we could provoke on it: DHCP outage, lost chip settings, stuck connections, all 17 single-register chip faults, a PHY held in reset and a link that stays down.
  • v3.3 behaves like a standard network device on the points that matter on a shared network: DHCP, address conflicts, unique MAC, no stuck connections, at most one ARP request a second. Its remaining standards gaps are listed above.
  • If a v3.3 unit ever drops off, its log will show why: link, chip, DHCP, address conflict, restart, and the supply voltage around each chip fault.

We can't say yet

  • That v3.3 ends the field dropouts: it recovers from the faults that match the symptom, but the trigger for static-mode units is still a hypothesis (supply sag) that needs a hands-on test.
  • How it handles a real deaf receiver: the new check is tested by emulation; the deaf state itself has only been seen on one board.
  • Why the network chip sometimes comes back deaf after a link negotiation (twice tonight, 38 s and about 3.5 min, both clearing by themselves), and whether it can last longer in the field.
  • How it behaves with real loads switching, over days, and on other switch brands.
  • Results rest on three boards and three days of testing; the same team wrote the firmware and the tests (mitigated by pre-registered criteria and crossover runs).

Recommended rollout

  1. Hands-on checks on the real box: inputs, factory reset and LED colours by hand; real power cycles with the USB cable unplugged.
  2. Supply-sag test (hands-on): the box on a lab supply, the 5 V rail sagged to 2.8–4.5 V and slow power-ups, v2.2 vs v3.3. Decides the leading hypothesis.
  3. Real loads: v3.3 on our showroom unit, loads switched while the log runs. Any chip re-init (with its supply voltage) in the log is a finding.
  4. Hardware, next board revision: brown-out level 4.3 V (fuse), a reset supervisor instead of the 22 µF capacitor, and the network chip's reset pin on a free GPIO.
  5. Pilot: v3.3 with per-unit MACs on units at sites that had dropouts. With no failure in N unit-days, the 95 % upper bound on the failure rate is about 3/N (≈ 300 unit-days to show fewer than one failure per 100 unit-days).
  6. Site settings: static addresses outside DHCP ranges, or DHCP reservations; EPS on an untagged access port in the AV VLAN.
  7. On any dropout, before power-cycling: note the LED colour, check whether the unit's MAC is still in the switch's MAC table, then read NET_LOG after it is back.
  8. Fallback: v2.2 can be re-flashed.
