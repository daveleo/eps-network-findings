Expromo EPS power sequencer · bench investigation

Supply sag is the trigger — and it needs a hardware fix

The field fault (a unit that stops answering the network until it is power-cycled) has now been reproduced on the bench with a programmable supply. A dip that pulls the board's 5 V rail into a narrow "no-man's-land" band leaves the Ethernet chip wedged — alive but deaf — until power is removed. We tested firmware, and we tested the original 22 µF reset capacitor: neither fixes it. The clean fix is a small hardware addition that resets the whole board whenever the supply leaves its safe range.

Bench, 2026-10-27 · one pilot box (firmware v3.3), RuiDeng RD6006 lab supply replacing the 10 V unit · supply controlled and the board read over the network · steps fixed before each run, every trial reported

What we found

A supply sag into roughly 2.7–3.8 V on the 5 V rail puts the W5500 Ethernet chip into a "deaf" state that only a power cycle clears. Above that band the board rides through; a very deep, clean loss of power reboots it cleanly. The danger is the shallow dip that lands in between.

This matches the field symptom exactly, and it matches the installation's wiring: the 10 V board supply shares its phase with solid-state relays that switch 30–40 switch-mode LED supplies each. The in-rush when those capacitor banks charge sags the shared phase at the very moment the box sequences its outputs on. The in-rush explanation is consistent with every bench result.

It cannot be fixed in firmware (the Ethernet chip's reset is on the shared board reset line, which software cannot drive, and a chip soft-reset does not clear the wedged state). The 22 µF reset capacitor does not fix it either. The fix is a reset-supervisor / power-good circuit, or decoupling the board's supply from the in-rush — see the hardware options.

The voltage danger band

The board runs from a 5 V regulator. Two parts have minimum voltages below which they misbehave; the chip's brown-out detector only forces a reset far lower, at a level the fuse reports as 2.7 V. Between the two sits a band where the parts run out of specification without a clean reset being forced — the recipe for a wedged chip.

Test 1 — power-up: rise time decides, and the capacitor does not save it

With the board powered only from the lab supply, we powered it down fully and brought it up at different rise times, checking each time whether it came up on the network. A near-instant step always worked; anything slower came up deaf. Fitting the original 22 µF reset capacitor did not change the slow-ramp result, and neither did a firmware change that waits for a healthy rail before starting the chip.

Test 2 — sag while running: the in-rush case

This is the closer match to the field. With the board up and healthy, we briefly dipped the input to a target for about 1.5 s, then restored 10 V, and recorded what happened. There is a sharp cliff between a dip that is ridden through and one that kills the board.

Why firmware and the capacitor can't fix it

Firmware can't

  • The Ethernet chip's reset pin is wired to the board's shared reset line, which the processor cannot drive — so software cannot truly reset the chip.
  • A chip soft-reset over the internal bus does not clear this wedged state (the self-check already does that and still failed to recover it).
  • A firmware build that waits for a healthy rail before starting the chip made no difference (0/3): the chip still leaves reset during the ramp, on its own line.

The 22 µF capacitor can't

  • It is a fixed delay on the reset line. It only helps a fast power-on, by holding reset through a rise of a few milliseconds.
  • On a slow ramp or a sag lasting tens of milliseconds or more, the delay is used up long before the rail is healthy, and the brown-out detector (2.7 V) lets the processor start into the danger band anyway.
  • Measured: with the cap fitted, the slow ramp was still 0/3, and a running dip to 4 V still went deaf.

The hardware fix — options to reconsider

  1. A reset-supervisor / power-good IC on the reset line (recommended). A small part (e.g. MCP809 / MCP130 / DS1813 class, ~4.5 V threshold) holds both the processor and the Ethernet chip in reset whenever the 5 V rail is below the threshold, and releases only once it is healthy — at any ramp speed. A sag then becomes an automatic clean reboot (the box restores its last state), instead of a dead unit. Unlike the bare capacitor it does not block USB programming.
  2. Hold-up energy, so the sag never reaches the board. A bulk capacitor — or a super-capacitor, or a small battery — behind a blocking diode on the board's 10 V input bridges the in-rush dip, keeping the rail up while the shared phase sags. This is the "large capacitor / battery" route: it is sound, and the size follows directly from how deep and how long the real sag is (which we should measure on site). A reset supervisor is still worth having as a backstop.
  3. Separate the board's supply from the in-rush. Feed the EPS board from a source that is not the same phase the output SSRs switch, so the in-rush cannot sag it. The cleanest root-cause fix where the wiring allows it.
  4. Future board revision: raise the brown-out level to 4.3 V (helps the processor, not the Ethernet chip on its own) and bring the Ethernet chip's reset out to a processor pin, so firmware can hard-reset it as a last resort.

Is the in-rush theory still valid?

Yes — it is consistent with every result, and nothing contradicts it. The bench shows that a sag into 2.7–3.8 V on the 5 V rail produces exactly the field symptom, and that it only takes a dip of tens of milliseconds. The installation shares the board's 10 V supply with the phase whose SSRs switch 30–40 switch-mode LED supplies; the capacitor-charging in-rush of those supplies is a credible source of such a sag, timed to when the box sequences power on — which is when units are reported to drop.

What is not yet proven is the last link in the chain: that the in-rush really pulls the rail into the danger band in the field. That needs a direct measurement on site — an oscilloscope on the 10 V rail, or the board's own logged minimum supply, captured while the SSRs switch their real loads. Until then the in-rush is the leading, well-supported explanation, not a closed case.

Honest limits

  • One pilot box, on the bench, with a lab supply standing in for the installed 10 V unit. The ramp and dip shapes are controlled approximations, not a recording of the real supply.
  • The rail thresholds (CPU 3.8 V, Ethernet chip 2.97 V, brown-out 2.7 V) are datasheet and fuse values; the deaf outcome and the current draw are measured on this board.
  • The field link — in-rush actually reaching the danger band on an installed system — is still to be measured on site.
  • Firmware v3.3 remains worthwhile: it rides through shallow sags and recovers link drops by itself. It simply cannot rescue the deep-sag deaf state, which is a hardware limit of this board.
