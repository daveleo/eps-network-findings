EPS: what the evidence supports, and what it doesn't

A desk review of the whole investigation: every document, the raw test logs, the bench scripts, the photos and the v3.3 firmware source. Every claim was traced back to its data. No hardware was connected and no new measurements were made. Where earlier conclusions, including today's, don't survive that check, this page says so.

2026-10-28 · Expromo Product Department · Sources: the private EPS repo (docs/, data/, tools/, photos/, the firmware) and the bench scripts of the supply study · Grades: Measured Contradicted by data Untested / unsupported Unknown Judgement

Executive summary

The current theory is a chain: switching 30–40 LED supplies per output → a few-millisecond mains dip → the COBI S-10-10 10 V supply sags → the 10 V comes back slowly → the W5500 hangs until power is removed → the field failure. Only two of those six links are established. The bench found a real, reproducible hardware weakness. Nobody has shown that the field produces it.

  1. The field failure has never been observed with an instrument. No failed unit has been examined in its failed state. The failure rate is unknown. The one customer report that was followed up turned out to be a different firmware (v1.9.1, 10-channel). Unknown
  2. The bench reproduced a real fault on one board. On the pilot box FriseH, a slow power-up ramp left the W5500 dead until power was removed in 26 of 26 trials, against 0 of 17 after a fast switch-on (exact p ≈ 2×10⁻¹²). Reset signals neither cleared it nor prevented it. Measured It was one board on one lab supply, and the voltage waveforms were never measured.
  3. Two numbers behind "a few milliseconds is enough" don't survive the raw data. First, the "100 ms ramp" was 20 verified Modbus writes, and its real length was never recorded. Second, the two "deaf after a running dip" results came from a script that declared a box dead 4 s after the dip. v3.3 needs about 9–11 s to answer after any reboot. With a 10 s wait, 9 of 9 dips just rebooted cleanly. Contradicted by data
  4. The report's "the supply holds the board about 0.9 s after a mains cut" contradicts its own log. Every cut from 0.22 s to 10 s gave the same ~5.6 s time-to-answer, which is a full reboot. So the real hold-up is under 0.22 s, and nothing shorter was ever measured. The same log shows 18 of 18 real COBI restarts came back by themselves (on unit 3, not FriseH). Contradicted by data
  5. A few-ms mains dip sagging the 10 V has never been tested. An SMPS running at about 20–30 % load normally rides through a sub-cycle dip. That is an engineering expectation, not a measurement. Untested
  6. Firmware: keep v3.3, retire v2.2. v3.3 removes real, measured v2.2 defects. It can't fix a W5500 that needs its power removed. On every reboot it switches all six outputs off, and they stay off unless "restore after reboot" is enabled (the default is off). Measured Code-read
  7. Hardware platform: the Keyestudio KS0304 has no published schematic. Its W5500 reset and power can't be controlled, the flash is 99.5 % used, and connections are hand-wired into headers. It can't be qualified to "does its task perfectly". Keep it for the installed base; don't base new production on it. Judgement

Bottom line. The supply theory is not dead, but it is not established either, and hardware development on top of it is premature. The two cheapest decisive experiments haven't been run:

  • Power the susceptible board (FriseH) from its own COBI supply through the Netio, and switch the mains 50–100 times.
  • Capture the 10 V of a loaded box when its walls switch on.

Until then, field behaviour should be observed before anyone power-cycles a dead box.

The theory as a chain, one link at a time

A causal chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Each link below is graded on what has actually been measured. Notes on the evidence follow each one.

  1. 1
    Each output switches 30–40 LED supplies (zero-cross SSRs)Fact

    This is the installation design: KANE KSSR-14840DA SSRs on A0–A5. The six outputs are sequenced, not simultaneous: OUT1 at 1.03 s, then one every ~0.3 s, all on at 2.52 s (report §5.4). So at any moment only one SSR's bank is starting up.

  2. 2
    That inrush dips the mains on the shared phase for a few msPlausible, never measured

    It is physically reasonable. Capacitor-input supplies draw a large charging current at switch-on, and zero-cross turn-on limits but doesn't remove it. The depth and length of the dip on site are unknown.

  3. 3
    The dip makes the COBI S-10-10's 10 V output sagUntested Supporting figure contradicted

    Nothing shorter than a 0.22 s mains cut was ever tested, and the "0.9 s hold-up" figure is wrong (see below). The supply is a 10 W unit running at roughly 2–3 W. Converters at that load normally carry their output through a sub-cycle interruption: 0.5–1 cycle dips are the standard immunity test for CE-marked equipment (EN 61000-4-11). That is an expectation, not a measurement of this unit.

  4. 4
    After the sag, the 10 V rises back slowlyUntested

    This link is what the fault needs. The bench shows a fast recovery only reboots the box, and that comes back by itself (9/9). In 18 real restarts of the COBI on unit 3 the box always came back, but that board was never shown to be susceptible.

  5. 5
    A slow rise leaves the W5500 dead until its power is removedMeasured, one board

    FriseH, slow staircase ramps from an RD6006: 26/26 dead. Board current while dead was 0.047–0.077 A against 0.18–0.20 A healthy, so the Ethernet chip isn't running. A reset pulse didn't recover it and holding reset through the ramp didn't prevent it. Removing power fixed it every time. Other boards were never ramp-tested.

  6. 6
    This is what customers experienceUnknown

    The field symptom is reported as "no ping, no web, no TCP until power-cycled". At least five bench mechanisms give that same symptom (below). Nobody has recorded the RJ45 link LED, the RGB colour, whether the walls stayed lit, or the switch-port state on a dead unit. Those four observations would separate the mechanisms.

What the bench actually shows

Established

  • On FriseH a slow rising supply reliably leaves the W5500 not running (low current, no network), and only removing power clears it.
  • A reset pulse doesn't clear it (Test 0a). Holding the board reset through the ramp doesn't prevent it (Test 0b, ×2). The 22 µF capacitor makes no difference on multi-second ramps. A firmware wait for a good rail makes no difference.
  • A dip followed by a fast recovery produces a clean reboot, not a hang.

Not established

  • The ramp speed at which the fault starts. Only "RD6006 switch-on" (never measured, assumed ≲ 20 ms) and staircases of roughly 0.2 s and longer were tested.
  • Whether other boards behave like FriseH.
  • Whether any real supply in the field rises slowly enough. FriseH itself has never been run from its own COBI supply.
  • What is physically stuck inside the W5500: the clock, the internal 1.2 V core regulator, or a reset path. Board current says "chip not running", not why.

Method errors and data corrections

1 · "Deaf after a running dip" was a waiting-time artefact

The 27 Oct script runsag.py restored 10 V, waited 4 s, and asked the box once (1 s timeout). With no answer it logged "DEAF (needs power cycle)" and power-cycled it straight away. So the box never had a chance to show whether it would come back. After any reboot, v3.3 answers only after ~9–11 s: it probes its address (RFC 5227) and negotiates the link twice. v2.2 answers after 2–3 s, so a 4 s window was valid for v2.2 but not for v3.3. The next day fast_dip.py checked at 4 s and again at 10 s, and every dip turned out to be a clean reboot. Its UPTIME was ~11 s at the first answer.

2 · The "100 ms" ramp wasn't 100 ms

ramprate_test.py builds a ramp from 20 calls to set_voltage() with sleep(0.1/20) between them. Each call is a Modbus write plus a verifying read over a CH340 USB-serial adapter, so 40 round-trips set the real length, not the 5 ms sleeps. The duration was never logged. It is almost certainly well above 100 ms; an estimate is 0.2–2 s. The page's claim that "a 100 ms rise is already too slow" therefore has no basis. That matters because real power supplies typically rise in tens of milliseconds, which is exactly the range that was never tested.

3 · The real supply does not hold the board 0.9 s

Report §5.15 states "the real box's supply holds the board ~0.9 s after a mains cut." The raw log data/m4250/events.csv says otherwise. Every cut, from 0.22 s to 10 s, was followed by the same ~5.6 s wait before the box answered. That equals a reboot plus the switch's ~3 s forwarding delay. Events that don't reboot the box (output switching, port flaps) answered within 0.8 s. So the box rebooted even on the shortest cut tested: real hold-up is under 0.22 s, and shorter cuts were never tried.

Firmware: what each failure does to the network and to the LED walls

For a power sequencer, "doing its task perfectly" ranks two failures differently. Outputs changing without a command (walls going dark on a live show) is worse than losing control (walls stay as they are, but can't be commanded). The firmware source shows that every reboot forces all six outputs off (setup()). They come back only if D5 is grounded or "restore after reboot" is enabled, and that setting defaults to off.

What the pilot can and can't show

The pilot runs three loaded boxes for at least two weeks: 42 box-days. If no failure is seen, that is only informative if failures were frequent before. The site's v2.2 failure history is item P5 in the pilot plan and is still unanswered. The canary's injected fault (a W5500 soft reset) demonstrates recovery from a fault class that has never been tied to the field.

Make the pilot informative (zero cost)

  1. Get the site's v2.2 history: how often, which boxes, when (after power-on? during a show? when the walls switch on?), DHCP or static.
  2. Before anyone power-cycles a dead box, note: the RJ45 link/activity LEDs, the status LED colour, whether its walls are lit, and the switch port's link state and MAC entry. Take a photo. Afterwards, pull NET_LOG.
  3. Make all four boxes identical in hardware (the 22 µF capacitor), or record exactly which ones differ.

Measure link 3 on site (low cost)

  • Put a meter with fast peak MIN capture (≈ 250 µs class) or a small USB scope on one loaded box's 10 V terminals. Run a POWER_ON and read the minimum.
  • If the 10 V doesn't move, the "few-ms dip" theory is refuted where it matters.
  • If it dips, the waveform shows its depth and the recovery speed: the two numbers any fix has to be sized for.

Product and user-experience findings

For the operator and the integrator

  • A reboot means dark walls. Outputs are off on every boot and stay off unless "restore after reboot" is on (the default is off). Whether the walls should come back by themselves after a power blip is a product decision. It hasn't been taken explicitly.
  • Slower comeback on v3.3: ~9–11 s to answer after a reboot, against 2–3 s on v2.2 (address probing plus a second link negotiation). Control systems with short timeouts will see this.
  • The status LED already tells "no network" (red with outputs off, yellow with all on), but operators aren't asked to report it.
  • Recovery is physical: a dead box needs someone at the cabinet to cut its power.

For the technician and production

  • Firmware update needs soldering when the 22 µF RESET capacitor is fitted: it blocks USB flashing and must be removed and refitted.
  • Per-unit builds for the MAC address (-DEPS_MAC_SUFFIX): a process where one slip gives two boxes the same MAC. A duplicate MAC has already been seen on the bench (report §5.6).
  • Factory reset means shorting D9 to GND for 10 s inside the box.
  • Flash 99.5 % used (162 B free): every future fix costs a feature.
  • Unverified (outside this review's scope): SSR thermal derating and capacitive-inrush rating for 30–40 supplies per channel; EMC/CE testing of the assembled box.

What the investigation did well

  • It pre-registered protocols, used crossover designs, reported every failure (including its own firmware's), gave exact confidence intervals, and openly retracted earlier conclusions. That is what made this review possible.
  • The v3.x network layer was built against measured defects, a 17-fault injection matrix and a 56-item standards suite. On the bench it dominates v2.2.
  • v3.3 logs the supply voltage and a W5500 "not responding" event into an EEPROM log that survives power cuts, so the pilot will produce evidence.
